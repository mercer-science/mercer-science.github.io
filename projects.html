Diffusion MRI pipeline and web app

UT Southwestern, Radiation Oncology. Built with the Claude Code AI agent.

Problem

The department's diffusion MRI processing pipeline had been passed down through several graduate students. When the last one left, it lived in his personal account on a single workstation, depended on fragile setups no one fully understood, and was about to be deleted. A postdoc hired for other work was inheriting it, and no one could run the earlier study without back-end access.

What I built

I rebuilt it into a web application. It gathers each patient's imaging from the hospital network shares, repairs broken files, runs the full chain of conversion, distortion correction, two neural-network denoising steps, model fitting, and day-to-day registration, and flags anything that looks wrong. A bulk runner queues many patients at once, schedules the work so the machine is not overloaded, archives finished patients automatically when disk space runs low, and exports everything in one download. On top of that sits a set of tools a physician or physicist can use directly: an image viewer, a parameter comparison view, a publication-figure creator, a tool that outlines treatment-resistant tissue and exports it to the planning system, and a voxel-level fit checker with quality statistics.

Result

It processed all seventy-six patients in one study in about two and a half days of server time; the earlier ten-patient study had taken about a week by hand. The hospital's IT group moved the application to the team that maintains clinical software, with a plan to run it department-wide, and the MRI vendor is interested in building the processing into the machine itself. I wrote the hand-off documentation and trained the postdoc who took it over.

Translating devices to the clinic

Two of my studies looked at machines that are only now reaching patients. Working around them taught me how a new device actually enters a clinic, which is slower and messier than a paper makes it look.

An AI agent for cryo-electron tomography

In the Jensen Lab I am testing whether a general-purpose AI coding agent can run the long cryo-ET processing pipeline while a human stays in charge of every scientific decision. It is the clearest example I have of using AI as an accelerator rather than a replacement, and it has its own write-up on the AI in Research page.

Lab instrumentation and thin films

In the Stowers Lab I helped build and keep running a flow reactor and an ultra-high-vacuum chamber, and I grew metal-organic framework thin films by spin coating, characterized them by electron microscopy, and trained new students on the process. That work is published in Langmuir; see Research.

Research tooling (open source)

Under the Mercer Science organization I build tools that help researchers use AI without losing control of their work.

  • paper-engine — skills and scripts that take an academic paper from a first idea toward a finished draft.
  • github-ai-project-manager — skills and hooks that let an AI command-line agent keep a research project organized on GitHub on its own.
  • AI-In-Research — the proposal behind the BYU AI Association's AI in Research group, which I lead.
