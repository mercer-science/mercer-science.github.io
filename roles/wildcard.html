Wild Card

The person who fills the gap the team did not plan for.

I keep ending up at the seams between fields: the bench and the clinic, the science and the software, the technology and the people it affects. A team working on something genuinely new usually has gaps it did not expect, and I am most useful filling them.

The Range

  • The bench. Two years of surface chemistry, building and running custom instruments, with a published paper, and now structural biology in a graduate-level lab.
  • The clinic. Clinical research in radiation oncology, plus time as a medical assistant, so I understand how care actually runs, not just how it is described.
  • The software. Applications and analysis pipelines, one of them now entering clinical use, built in part with AI coding agents.
  • The policy side. A data sovereignty working table at BYU's AI for Social Innovation Conference, thinking about who owns community data and who benefits from it.

Connecting Them Is the Point

These are not four separate hobbies. In one summer at UT Southwestern I worked at the bench understanding a new machine, wrote the software to analyze it, sat with physicians to design around their decisions, and thought about how the tool would reach patients at all. The pipeline I built only matters if it reaches a clinician in a few minutes rather than an hour, and closing that gap is both an engineering problem and a human one. That overlap is exactly what I want to work in, and it is why I am pursuing an MD-PhD.

Leading and Serving

I lead the AI in Research meeting for the BYU AI Association, I direct an early-childhood literacy program through Y-Serve, and I have trained lab members, a postdoc, and incoming students. I am comfortable both leading a group and being the newest person on a hard team.
