iGEM / Life Science

A biochemist who can also build the team's software and models.

iGEM teams design and build genetic systems, then have to characterize them, model them, and account for how the work lands in the real world. I would come in with solid life-science fundamentals and the computational skills that these teams most often go looking for.

The Science Training

I am a biochemistry major on BYU's standard biochemistry track, and I have worked through the major's chemistry and biochemistry sequence to CHEM 584, which covers advanced biochemistry on top of organic and physical chemistry. I am taking molecular biology coursework this year as well. More to the point, I already work in a graduate-level research lab, sharing instruments and problems with master's and doctoral students rather than watching from the side. I know how to learn an unfamiliar technique quickly and how to keep careful records while doing it.

What I Would Add to the Team

  • Software and automation. I build applications and analysis pipelines for a living, including one now entering clinical use. iGEM projects generate a lot of data and usually need someone who can wrangle it; that is my strongest contribution.
  • Modeling and analysis. I work in Python and R and have built reproducible pipelines that turn raw measurements into figures and models without hand-editing along the way.
  • Hands at the bench. Two years of wet-lab work means I can pull my weight on the experimental side, not only the computer.
  • Human practices. iGEM takes the ethics and community side seriously, and so do I. I sat on a data sovereignty working table at BYU's AI for Social Innovation Conference, thinking about who owns community data and who benefits from it. That is the same muscle iGEM's human-practices work asks for.

Being Honest About Fit

My research has not been genetic engineering, so I would be learning the synthetic-biology techniques alongside the team. What I bring instead is the foundation to pick them up fast and a set of skills, mostly on the computational and tool-building side, that complete a team rather than duplicate it. One area I would be excited to work on is diagnostics for infectious disease, which connects to the bacterial imaging I already do in the Jensen Lab.
