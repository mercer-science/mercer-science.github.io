Research & Publications

Three fields, one habit: build the tool, then answer the question.

My research has run across surface chemistry, radiation oncology, and structural biology. The common thread is that each project needed a piece of software or instrumentation built before the science could move, so I ended up building it. Below is the work by field, followed by the full publication list.

Radiation Oncology — UT Southwestern Medical Center

Clinical research assistant with Dr. Kenneth Westover and Dr. Jie Deng, May 2026 to present. In person in Dallas through the summer, remote since.

I joined through BYU's Summer Premedical Research Internship and led three retrospective studies of new radiation therapy technology. The projects shared one obstacle: clinical data is messy and inconsistent. Physicians name anatomical structures differently, backups get skipped, and simulation and treatment happen on different scanners. Most of my work was writing tools that cleaned up that mess automatically, then using the clean data to ask a clinical question.

Biology-guided radiotherapy on the RefleXion machine

Question

RefleXion is a new machine that uses a tumor's own PET signal to steer the radiation beam in real time. It is commercially stalled because there is not yet clear evidence of benefit, so we studied whether it delivers what it plans and how it compares with standard image-guided radiotherapy.

What I did

I wrote a C# script that pulls dose data for every patient out of the treatment planning system despite the inconsistent naming, with Python and R steps that build every figure and table from the raw export automatically. When a physicist raised a concern that organ motion could be distorting the results, I reconstructed each patient's respiratory motion from backup-plan contours and ran roughly a thousand geometric checks per patient to test it. Our organ-dose findings were taken to the manufacturer around the summer physics meeting.

In final circulation among co-authors, then submission.

Diffusion MRI on the Elekta Unity MR-Linac (lung)

Question

The MR-Linac quietly records diffusion-weighted MRI before each treatment, but no one used it clinically. We asked whether that on-board imaging can show, during treatment, where a lung tumor is still active.

What I did

I processed ten patients, each with roughly ten gigabytes of poorly organized imaging, and reframed the study into three honest aims when the data would not support the original one. While making routine quality-control histograms I noticed values piling up at a single number and traced it to a bug that was capping a key measurement in the exporter; I fixed the pipeline and re-ran everything. The main result is a tool that outlines the treatment-resistant core of a tumor so a physician could give that region an extra dose.

Submitted to the International Journal of Radiation Oncology, Biology, Physics (the "Red Journal").

A radiation-transparent patient frame (AerFrame)

Question

Dr. Westover invented and patented a patient immobilization frame made of a composite that is nearly invisible to radiation. A physics study was needed to show how much dose the frame saves compared with conventional frames.

What I did

No database held the right cases, so I hand-screened about fifty patient records to find eleven that fit, chosen to mirror real practice across tumor sites and treatment types. I ran the attenuation calculations with the physicists, added bench measurements with an ion chamber, and wrote the first draft. The frame delivered more of the planned dose to the target, with the largest gains where the target sits close to the body surface.

In final circulation among co-authors, then submission.

Diffusion MRI in pancreatic SBRT

Question

Dr. Deng brought me onto a study asking whether the same diffusion measurements track how pancreatic tumors respond to stereotactic radiation. It is where the lung project's exploratory tool starts to be tested against real outcomes.

What I did

I ran all seventy-six patients through the pipeline I had rebuilt and did the chart review for response and failure. An early signal is promising: a patient who later failed treatment showed diffusion trending the wrong way within the first few fractions, early enough that a physician could in principle have changed the plan.

In progress (chart review and analysis).

Structural Biology — Jensen Lab, BYU

Undergraduate researcher, August 2026 to present. PI: Dr. Grant Jensen.

Cryo-electron tomography freezes cells and images them from many angles to reconstruct what is inside at near-molecular detail. The processing is a long chain of specialized tools with a steep learning curve. My main project asks whether a general-purpose AI coding agent can shorten that curve by proposing, installing, and running each step while a human approves or overrides every decision. I also work on imaging bacteria associated with tick-borne disease. I presented the AI work as a poster at the Utah CryoEM Symposium and described it on the AI in Research page.

Surface Chemistry — Stowers Lab, BYU

Chemistry research assistant, April 2024 to May 2026. PI: Dr. Kara Stowers.

I helped build, repair, and run custom instruments, including a flow reactor and an ultra-high-vacuum chamber with X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy and mass spectrometry, reaching about one ten-billionth of atmospheric pressure. I grew and characterized metal-organic framework thin films by spin coating, imaged them on a scanning electron microscope, and trained newer students on both. That work produced a published paper, with a second on the vacuum-chamber chemistry in preparation.
