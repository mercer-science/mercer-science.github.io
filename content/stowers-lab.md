### Problem

Metal-organic frameworks are crystalline, porous materials with enormous internal surface
area, which makes them interesting for catalysis and separations. Growing them as thin
films attached to a surface usually needs a self-assembled monolayer to nucleate on, which
adds a step and limits what substrates you can use. We wanted films that grow on a bare
substrate, at ambient pressure, with control over how a second metal gets incorporated.

### Solution

I synthesized copper and copper-zinc framework films by spin coating, cycling metal and
linker solutions onto the substrate, then characterized them on a scanning electron
microscope and measured film thickness in ImageJ. Alongside the films I helped build,
repair, and maintain the lab's two custom instruments: a flow reactor for catalysis runs
and an ultra-high-vacuum chamber that reaches about 10⁻¹⁰ Torr, carrying X-ray
photoelectron spectroscopy and a mass spectrometer. Keeping that chamber running meant leak
checking, bakeouts, flange work, and rebuilding the spin coater's vacuum system when it
failed. I trained two newer students in spin coating and electron microscopy.

### Result

The work is published in *Langmuir*, where I am second author, and I presented it at the
BYU Student Research Conference in 2025 and 2026 and at the American Chemical Society
spring meeting in Atlanta in March 2026. Temperature turned out to control coverage and
crystallite size in ways the figures above show directly. A second manuscript, on the
decomposition chemistry studied in the vacuum chamber, is in preparation, and I still help
finish it although my position ended in May 2026.
