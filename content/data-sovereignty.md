At the AI for Social Innovation Conference in October 2026 I sat at the Data Sovereignty
and Inclusion working table as the BYU AI Association's representative. The people around
it included a UN ambassador who chairs work on science and technology for development, the
chief operating officer of a Māori media organization that built a community-owned speech
model, and executives from industry.

The problems the table named are concrete. Communities whose data and languages train these
systems rarely control them or see any of the value. Training data skews Western. Research
teams arrive in a community, take its data, publish, and leave. Principles exist in
abundance, but almost none have been turned into terms a community and a company could both
sign and keep.

I helped start two pieces of follow-up work: a short op-ed that puts the problem in plain
language, and a draft agreement setting out who may use what data, for what purpose, who
decides, what returns to the community, and how a community says no. The group means to
take a preliminary draft to the UN and to private developers, and the work continues.

The line from the table I keep coming back to is that data is not the new oil but the new
soil, which is a different claim about what sovereignty is for. For me it connects directly
to the clinical work: the imaging I processed at UT Southwestern came from patients who
will never see the paper, and the question of what the people who generate data are owed
does not stay abstract for long.
