Three of my projects sat on machines or devices that are only now reaching patients, which
taught me more about how medical technology actually arrives than any paper has.

The RefleXion machine is FDA-cleared and installed at a handful of U.S. centers, and it is
commercially stalled for want of evidence that it helps. Our study is part of that
evidence, and it says the technology earns its place for tumors that move a lot and not
much elsewhere. A physicist on the team carried our organ-dose findings to the
manufacturer, which is revising its post-treatment evaluation.

The AerFrame is a patented device invented by the physician I worked for, in use at one
center and heading to others, and the study I designed is the physics case that will travel
with it.

The diffusion MRI application is the one I built. It has moved from a research workstation
to the team that runs clinical software, and the MRI vendor has asked about putting the
processing on the machine itself. The gap between an hour per patient and the three to five
minutes a clinic allows is the whole problem, and watching three technologies sit in that
gap at once is why I want to work there.
