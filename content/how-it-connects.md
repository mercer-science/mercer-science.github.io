The short version: I keep ending up in places where the science could not move until
somebody built the tool, and I would rather be the one who builds it than the one who
waits.

**Chemistry taught me instruments.** Two years in a surface chemistry lab meant leak
checking a vacuum chamber at 10⁻¹⁰ Torr, rebuilding a spin coater's vacuum system when it
died, and running a flow reactor. Nothing in that work ran because someone handed me a
working machine. You fix it, or you have no data.

**Biology gave me the question I care about.** In Dr. Jensen's lab I scoped two
cryo-electron tomography projects on bacteria and a parasite, both waiting on biosafety
approval. Cryo-ET answers a specific kind of question: what does this machinery actually
look like, in place, in a cell nobody fixed or stained. A published claim about *Bartonella*
rests on phenotypes alone, and the tomogram would either support it or not.

**The clinic showed me the gap.** At UT Southwestern I rebuilt a diffusion MRI pipeline
into an application that now belongs to the team running clinical software. The research
version takes an hour per patient. A clinical version has three to five minutes. I spent a
summer learning that a result which cannot reach the console on time is not yet medicine,
which is the single clearest reason I want to train as a physician-scientist.

**AI is the lever, and it needs watching.** I built that application with a coding agent in
weeks, and I am now running a controlled test in the Jensen Lab of where an agent helps a
newcomer through a hard pipeline and where it quietly gets it wrong. Both results are worth
having. I lead the weekly AI in Research meeting for the BYU AI Association for the same
reason.

**Policy is where it lands.** At BYU's AI for Social Innovation Conference I sat at a
working table on data sovereignty with a UN ambassador and people from industry and
indigenous media, helping start an op-ed and a draft agreement about who controls community
data. The imaging I processed came from patients who will never see the papers. The
question of what people are owed for the data that trains these systems is not abstract to
me.

Chemistry, biology, the clinic, code, and policy are not five interests. They are the five
places one problem shows up: good science stalls where the tooling, the translation, or the
trust runs out.
