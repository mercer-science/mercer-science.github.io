AI in Research

Using AI as an accelerator, and being honest about where it stops.

A lot of my recent work has been figuring out what AI coding agents can and cannot do in real research. I have used them to build software I could not have built alone in the time I had, and I have watched them fail in ways that matter. Both sides are the point.

Can an AI agent shorten the cryo-ET learning curve?

Jensen Lab, BYU. Poster presented at the Utah CryoEM Symposium, October 2026.

Cryo-electron tomography turns thousands of tilted images of a frozen cell into a three-dimensional map of its interior. Getting there means stringing together a dozen specialized programs, each with its own settings and failure modes, and learning it normally takes months. For my main Jensen Lab project I asked a narrower, testable version of a big question: can a general-purpose AI coding agent carry a newcomer through that pipeline?

The setup keeps the human in charge. On a set of tilt series of growing E. coli cells, the agent proposes each step, installs and configures the tool, and runs it, while I approve, override, or correct every parameter. Each recommendation is logged as accepted, overridden, or an error I caught, so we can measure where the agent genuinely helps and where it needs a human who already knows the science. The pipeline runs from motion correction through alignment, reconstruction, template matching, and averaging, ending in an analysis of how ribosomes cluster inside the cell. A manuscript is in preparation.

AI-built clinical software

At UT Southwestern I rebuilt a diffusion MRI processing pipeline into a web application using the Claude Code agent, and it is now moving into clinical use. That project is described in full under Projects. The honest version of the story: the agent let me build, in a few weeks, infrastructure that would otherwise have taken far longer, but the scientific judgment and the narrative had to come from me. Our research was new enough that there were few existing patterns for the agent to draw on, which is exactly where this kind of tool is weakest.

Leading the conversation at BYU

I am an officer of the BYU AI Association, where I lead the weekly AI in Research meeting: a group working through how researchers across fields can use these tools well. I wrote the proposal for the group, which is public as the AI-In-Research repository, and I build open tooling toward the same goal under Mercer Science.

Who owns the data

As the AI Association's representative I joined a data sovereignty working table at BYU's AI for Social Innovation Conference, alongside people from the United Nations, industry, and communities whose data trains these systems. The question was who owns community data, the models built from it, and the value they create. I helped start two pieces of follow-up work: a short public piece explaining the problem in plain terms, and a draft agreement setting out how communities, developers, and governments might share that data on fair terms. The work is continuing.

A worry worth stating

I am genuinely optimistic about what these tools do for research, and I also think they change how we think, not only what we produce. As more writing and reasoning gets handed to AI, the average gets more capable while the individual risks getting less practiced: less nuance, less argument, less of the slow work that understanding is made of. Holding both of those at once, the acceleration and the cost, is why I would rather help shape how AI enters science than just use it.
